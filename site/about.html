About

Quantum Mechanics tells us that the atoms in a material are never still; they are always vibrating with energy. Phonons are used to describe these vibrations. They are important because they affect a whole host of material properties, such as stability, heat transport, light absorption and electronic conductivity.

Every material has its own set of vibration frequencies, determined by the types and arrangements of atoms in the material. For example, light atoms and stiff bonds vibrate fast; heavy atoms and soft bonds vibrate slowly. This website sonifies these vibrations, allowing you to hear the unique acoustic signature of each material.

What you are hearing

Phonons vibrate around a trillion times a second (terahertz, THz), about 35 octaves above anything you can hear. Each sound on this site takes those frequencies and plays them 35 octaves lower, so 1 THz sounds at about 29 Hz.

A material vibrates across a range of frequencies, at a density given by the phonon density of states (DOS). This website fits the spectrum with a handful of Gaussian peaks, and maps the features of each peak to audible parameters.

The peak fits are a simplification: they keep the main features of each spectrum and lose the fine detail. The chart on the Listen page shows the full density of states with the fitted peaks over it.

The sounds

  • chord: every peak sounds together; taller peaks are louder, broader peaks have more vibrato.
  • arp: the peaks sound one after another, low to high; taller peaks are louder, sharper peaks ring longer.
  • pluck: the chord struck once; taller peaks are louder, sharper peaks ring longer.
  • spectrum: each peak as a band of noise; taller peaks are louder, broader peaks have a wider bandwidth.

Elements and temperature

In a material made of more than one element, each element takes part in the vibrations differently: typically, light atoms dominate the high frequencies and heavy atoms the low ones.

With no temperature chosen you hear every vibration the crystal can have. At 300 K (room temperature) or 600 K, each vibration is weighted by how many phonons are actually present at that temperature (the Bose–Einstein occupation). Low frequencies are easy to excite, so they become louder.

How it is made

The phonon data comes from the Materials Project, calculated with density functional theory. The peaks are fitted with SingingMaterials and turned into Strudel code with MP2Strudel. Strudel plays the sound in your browser. The code is shown under every sound; open it in strudel.cc to change it and make your own.
