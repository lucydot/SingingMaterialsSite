About

The atoms in a material are never still - they are always vibrating with energy. These vibrations are important because they affect a wide range of material properties, such as stability, heat transport, light absorption and electronic conductivity.

Each material has its own set of vibrational frequencies, determined by the types and arrangements of atoms. For example, light atoms and short, stiff bonds vibrate fast; heavy atoms and long, soft bonds vibrate slowly. This website sonifies these vibrations, so that you can hear the unique vibrational fingerprint of each material.

What you are hearing

Materials vibrate around a trillion times a second (terahertz, THz), which is well above anything a human can hear. The algorithm underlying this site takes those frequencies and plays them 35 octaves lower, so 1 THz sounds at about 29 Hz, within the audible range.

A material vibrates across a range of frequencies, as specified by the density of states (DOS). States refers to phonons, the quantum mechanical vibrations of the crystal. The DOS is a spectrum showing how many phonons exist at each frequency. This website fits the spectrum with a series of Gaussian peaks, and maps the features of each peak to audible parameters.

The sounds

  • chord: every peak sounds together; taller peaks are louder, broader peaks have more vibrato.
  • arp: the peaks sound one after another, low to high; taller peaks are louder, sharper peaks ring longer.
  • pluck: the chord struck once; taller peaks are louder, sharper peaks ring longer.
  • spectrum: each peak as a band of noise; taller peaks are louder, broader peaks have a wider bandwidth.

Elements and temperature

In a material made of more than one element, each element contributes tothe vibrational spectrum differently: typically, light atoms dominate the high frequencies and heavy atoms the low ones.

With no temperature chosen you hear every vibration the crystal can have. At 300 K (room temperature) or 600 K, each vibration is weighted by how many vibrational excitations (phonons) are actually present at that temperature (the Bose–Einstein occupation). Low frequencies are easy to excite, so they become louder.

Key limitations of this approach

The peak fits are a simplification: in most cases, they keep the main features of each spectrum but they do lose the fine detail. The chart on the Listen page shows the full density of states with the fitted peaks over it.

The temperature-dependence ignores higher-order effects such as thermal expansion or phonon-phonon interactions.

How it is made

The density of states data comes from the Materials Project, calculated with Density Functional Theory. The peaks are fitted with SingingMaterials and turned into Strudel code with MP2Strudel. Strudel plays the sound in your browser. The code is shown under every sound; open it in strudel.cc to adapt it and play your own compositions.
